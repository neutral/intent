# Explain a decision heuristic

Use this optional approach when the Discipline helps a worker choose between
plausible alternatives. Teach the forces that matter and the signals that favor
different answers. Useful advice transfers the reasoning needed to make a choice
when the situation differs from the author's examples.

## Develop the guidance

Identify the recurring decision and why it matters. Explain the difficulty the
heuristic addresses and the consequence of a poor choice. Describe the conditions
where this advice is useful and the information a worker needs before applying
it. Keep important exclusions close to the guidance they qualify.

Expose the competing concerns. Explain what each alternative gains, costs, or
leaves uncertain. Identify cues a worker can actually observe: dependencies that
must change together, repeated need to understand the same concept, costly
reversibility, or evidence that an abstraction is premature. Choose cues relevant
to the practice rather than presenting a universal decision matrix.

Explain how context changes the answer. A heuristic can identify a useful default
while acknowledging exceptions. Describe what would justify an exception and
which information would make the choice worth revisiting. Avoid converting a
preference into an unsupported rule through words such as “always.”

Describe costs of misapplication. A worker can follow the literal advice while
defeating its purpose. Explain the warning signs and how to reconsider the
decision. Distinguish evaluating whether the decision was reasoned from later
evidence about whether it produced the intended benefit.

## What richer information adds

“Prefer small changes” can teach a worker to group work around a coherent decision,
keep the explanation reviewable, and identify independently useful steps. It also
needs to address coupled protocol changes where splitting the work would create
an invalid intermediate state. The useful reasoning concerns review effort,
coherence, and recoverability; a line-count rule cannot settle the tradeoff.

“Introduce an abstraction when code repeats” can conceal whether the repetitions
share a stable concept or merely similar syntax. Richer guidance can compare
independent change patterns, caller needs, and the cost of coupling them. A worker
then has reasons to extract shared meaning in one situation and tolerate local
duplication in another.

A comparison table or contrasting situations may help. Explain the causal
reason behind each comparison so the worker can apply it without reproducing a
fixed scoring system. If the advice rests on limited experience, keep that basis
visible rather than presenting imagined evidence of broad success.

## Judge the result

A worker should recognize the decision, identify the relevant forces, explain
their choice, and know what could change it. Expand where the reader would need
the author's unstated judgment.
