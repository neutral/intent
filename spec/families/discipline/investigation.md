# Explain an investigation practice

Use this optional approach when the Discipline helps a worker reduce uncertainty
about a problem, cause, or opportunity. Teach how to distinguish plausible
explanations and choose informative next observations. A useful investigation
practice explains what each step can teach and how that learning changes the
next move.

## Develop the guidance

Identify the question and triggering situation. Describe what is known, what is
reported or assumed, and what remains uncertain. Explain the information needed
to begin and the conditions under which this practice is premature or unsuitable.
A worker should not have to invent the investigation's purpose from a list of
tools or actions.

Explain how to form and compare hypotheses. Encourage explanations that account
for the observed facts and make different predictions. Show how an observation
can discriminate among them, and distinguish evidence against an explanation
from failure to find supporting evidence. Keep the process open to a cause that
the initial list did not include.

Explain how to choose the next step. Useful considerations can include expected
information, cost, reversibility, available access, and effects on the system
being investigated. Describe the reason for a sequence where order matters.
Advice about an external action does not authorize that action.

Include stopping and recovery. Explain when the evidence supports a bounded
conclusion, when another owner or missing environment is needed, and when the
approach should change. Describe how to retain uncertainty honestly after an
inconclusive examination. Avoid treating a plausible narrative as a confirmed
cause merely because it fits the initial symptoms.

## What richer information adds

“Reproduce a bug before fixing it” becomes useful when the Discipline explains
that a failing example can distinguish a suspected cause from neighboring
explanations and reveal whether a change addresses it. It can also explain why
a reproduction built from the same mistaken assumptions offers weak evidence.

When the reported environment is unavailable, the advice can suggest tracing
the claimed failure path, identifying the missing condition, and limiting the
confidence of a proposed fix. A missing reproduction should not be disguised as
a successful examination. Nor should the guide imply that every investigation
has one fixed sequence regardless of the available evidence.

For a performance investigation, richer guidance can separate a suspected
bottleneck from an observed one, identify the boundary measured, and explain why
changing workload can alter the inference. The purpose is transferable reasoning;
the Discipline need not prescribe a particular measurement product.

A hypothesis table, worked investigation, or short decision tree can help when
it exposes how observations change the next step. Keep the basis and limits of
claims visible, including when the examples are illustrative.

## Judge the result

A worker should be able to begin with a clear question, select an informative
observation, update their explanation, and state what remains unknown. The guide
should make both progress and honest stopping possible.
